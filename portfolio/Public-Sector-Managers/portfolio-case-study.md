# Case Study: Designing a Virtual Workshop for Public Sector Managers

**Project Type:** Instructional Design Assignment
**Context:** Interview assignment for a learning consultancy
**Duration:** 1 week
**Format:** 2-hour virtual session
**Audience:** New public sector managers

---

## The Challenge

A learning consultancy requested a complete instructional design for a 2-hour virtual workshop targeting new public sector managers. The session needed to equip participants with practical skills for navigating team communication during organizational change — specifically addressing resistance, institutional barriers, and difficult conversations.

**Key constraints:**
- **Virtual delivery only** — no in-person fallback
- **Diverse experience levels** — new managers with varying backgrounds
- **Time pressure** — meaningful skill development in just 120 minutes
- **Public sector context** — hierarchical structures, bureaucratic constraints, decisions made elsewhere
- **Application focus** — participants needed to leave with skills they could use immediately, not just theoretical knowledge

The design needed to demonstrate rigorous application of adult learning theory while producing deliverables ready for real-world implementation.

---

## The Approach

### 1. Framework Evaluation

Rather than defaulting to a familiar communication model, I evaluated two research-backed frameworks against the specific needs of public sector managers:

- **Crucial Conversations** (Patterson et al.)
- **Three Conversations** (Stone, Patton & Heen, Harvard)


I built a weighted evaluation matrix scoring each framework across five criteria:
- Cognitive load (simpler = better for anxious new managers)
- Research backing (credibility matters in government)
- Power dynamics (managers often communicate decisions they didn't make)
- Feelings normalization (government culture suppresses emotion)
- Public sector fit (does it account for bureaucratic realities?)

**Decision:** Three Conversations framework scored 8.65/10. Its three-element structure (What Happened, Feelings, Identity) carried lower cognitive load than Crucial Conversations' seven-step model, and it directly addressed the power asymmetry inherent in public sector management.

### 2. Learning Theory Application

I grounded the design in **Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle**, which requires learning to move through four stages:
1. **Concrete Experience** — encountering something
2. **Reflective Observation** — stepping back to process
3. **Abstract Conceptualization** — forming understanding
4. **Active Experimentation** — applying in practice

Most training skips Reflective Observation by jumping directly from experience to conceptualization. I designed **four explicit reflection pauses** totaling 12 minutes (10% of session time) to prevent this violation.

I also applied **Knowles' andragogy principles**: adult learners need relevance, autonomy, and respect for their existing knowledge. The design honors this through realistic scenarios, learner choice in practice rounds, and peer feedback rather than top-down evaluation.

### 3. Pre-Work as Pattern Discovery

I created a pre-work case study featuring Alex Chen, a social work supervisor announcing a state-mandated system change to a resistant team. The case was deliberately designed to **violate all three conversations**: Alex argues for one truth, suppresses feelings, and gets defensive when challenged.

Learners arrive at the session having already identified patterns that don't work. Module 1 surfaces those observations and transitions directly into the framework — making the introduction feel discovered, not delivered.

## The Solution

I delivered six integrated components:

### 1. Session Agenda
A high-level 2-hour agenda structured around Kolb's cycle:

| Module | Time | Focus | Kolb Stage |
|--------|------|-------|------------|
| 1 — Opening & Context | 0:00–0:20 | Case analysis, pattern extraction | CE, RO |
| 2 — Framework | 0:20–0:50 | Three Conversations intro, video demo | AC |
| 3 — Practice | 0:50–1:30 | Two triad role-play rounds | AE |
| 4 — Barriers | 1:30–1:50 | Institutional constraints, peer strategies | AC |
| 5 — Integration | 1:50–2:00 | Action planning, accountability | AE |

**Practice is the largest block** — 40 minutes, one-third of the session. Skill development requires doing, not just understanding.

### 3. Pre-Recorded Video Script
A 4-5 minute video demonstration showing Jordan (new manager) and Priya (senior analyst) navigating a performance conversation about missed deadlines. The video:
- Shows the **Identity Conversation as internal voiceover** (Jordan's pre-meeting preparation)
- Demonstrates all three conversations correctly applied
- Uses a **one-on-one scenario** (different from the group announcement in the case study) to show framework transferability
- Includes production notes and an SME review checklist

**Why video instead of live role-play?** In a 2-hour virtual session, live role-play carries real facilitation risk — volunteer reluctance, unpredictable quality, setup time. A video is reliable, controllable, and accessible with captions.

## Key Design Decisions

### Decision 1: Reflection as Non-Negotiable
Kolb's Reflective Observation stage is the most commonly skipped element in training design. I made it explicit and non-negotiable with four timed pauses:

| Pause | Time | Reflects on |
|-------|------|-------------|
| 1 | 0:05–0:08 | Pre-work case before pair discussion |
| 2 | 0:35–0:38 | Video demonstration before Q&A |
| 3 | 1:02–1:04 | Practice round before peer feedback |
| 4 | 1:50–1:53 | Whole session before action planning |

**Why it matters:** Silence on Zoom feels awkward. It's also where experience converts to insight.

### Decision 2: Two-Scenario Design
The pre-work case study (group announcement) and the video demonstration (one-on-one performance conversation) use **different contexts intentionally**. This shows learners the framework isn't just for one type of conversation — it transfers across situations.

### Decision 3: Practice Before Perfection
Module 3 includes **two practice rounds** with structured feedback between them. Learners try the framework, receive peer observations, reflect, and try again. This is deliberate practice (Ericsson), not just activity.

### Decision 4: SME Collaboration Points
The design identifies six SME touchpoints:
1. Case study scenario validation (is Alex's situation realistic?)
2. Practice scenario development (what conversations do managers actually face?)
3. Video script review (does Jordan's approach feel authentic?)
4. Module 4 institutional barriers brainstorm (what constraints matter most?)
5. Video production (need two performers + editor)
6. Pilot facilitation feedback (what worked, what flopped?)

**Why it matters:** Instructional designers don't work in isolation. Good design requires subject matter expertise, especially for context-specific content like public sector management.

## Outcomes

- **Demonstrated rigor** — framework evaluation matrix, explicit Kolb mapping, and SME collaboration planning showed depth beyond AI-generated submissions

## Reflections

This project reinforced three principles I bring to all instructional design work:

**1. Theory informs practice, but context drives decisions.**
I didn't choose Three Conversations because it's trendy — I chose it because it fit the specific needs of public sector managers navigating power asymmetry.

**2. Reflection isn't optional.**
Kolb's cycle isn't complete without Reflective Observation. Most training skips it. Good training makes time for it.

**3. Design is collaborative, not solitary.**
The five SME touchpoints weren't extras — they were essential to creating content that would actually land with the target audience.
